Music theory, from the ground up

Start with notes, build chords, then use them to make music your own.

Start with sound

Music theory is a set of names for sounds and patterns. It helps you understand what you hear; it is not a set of rules you must follow.

Notes and octaves

The note names repeat: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, then A again. The same note higher or lower is an octave apart.

On a piano, find any C. The next C has the same name and sounds higher.

Half steps and whole steps

A half step is the distance to the very next piano key. A whole step is two half steps. These small distances build scales and chords.

C to C# is a half step. C to D is a whole step.

Sharps, flats, and natural notes

A sharp raises a note by a half step. A flat lowers it by a half step. A natural sign cancels a sharp or flat.

C# and Db use different names for the same piano key.

Keeping time

A beat is the steady pulse you tap your foot to. The time signature tells you how beats are grouped. In 4/4, count 1, 2, 3, 4 and repeat.

Try clapping on 1 and 3 while counting all four beats.

Scales are note families

A scale is a small family of notes. Pick one note as home, then follow a pattern of steps to visit the other notes.

The major scale pattern

A whole step means move past one piano key. A half step means move to the very next key. Major scales use this pattern:

WWHWWWH

Start on C: C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C. This one uses only the white keys.

The natural minor scale pattern

Minor scales use a different step pattern. The natural minor pattern is:

WHWWHWW

Start on A: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A. It uses the same white keys as C major, but A feels like home.

Scale degrees

Give the notes numbers 1 through 7. Note 1 is home. Note 5 often points back home, and note 7 sits right beside it.

In C major, C is 1, G is 5, and B is 7.

Major and minor

Major and minor are two different ways to space the notes. Major often feels bright; minor often feels darker or more thoughtful.

The notes can be the same, but changing which note feels like home changes the sound.

Choose notes that fit

When learning a song, start by using notes from its scale. You can then add other notes for color, as long as you listen to how they feel.

Chords combine notes

A chord is a few notes played together. Chords built from a song's scale tend to fit naturally with one another.

Build a triad

A basic three-note chord uses a root, a third, and a fifth. The root gives it its name.

C major = C, E, G. C minor = C, Eb, G.

Major, minor, and diminished

Major triads stack a larger third then a smaller third. Minor triads reverse that order. Diminished triads sound especially tense.

Major: 4 then 3 half steps. Minor: 3 then 4. Diminished: 3 then 3.

Roman numerals

Roman numerals show a chord's place in a key. Uppercase means major; lowercase means minor. A small circle marks diminished.

In C major: I = C, ii = D minor, V = G, vi = A minor.

Inversions

An inversion uses the same chord notes but puts a different one at the bottom. This can make chord changes smoother.

C-E-G and E-G-C are both C major; the second has E in the bass.

Three useful roles: I is home, ii or IV moves away, and V creates a pull that often resolves to I.

Make chord progressions

A progression is a repeating order of chords. Good progressions create a feeling of movement, then give the listener a place to rest.

Try a progression in another key

    Listen for a story

    Start on a home chord, move to a chord that opens things up, add a little tension, then return home. Repeating the loop helps it feel like a song.

    Start with these patterns

    I - IV - V - I sounds settled and familiar. I - V - vi - IV is common in pop. ii - V - I gives a clear pull home and is common in jazz.

    How to find your own

    Choose a key, try chords I, IV, V, and vi, and move them into an order that feels good. There is no single correct progression; your ears are the final check.

    Make changes smoother

    Keep notes that chords share, and move the other notes by the smallest distance you can. This is called voice leading, but the idea is simply: don't make every note jump.

    Improvise one small idea at a time

    Improvising is making up a melody in the moment. You do not need lots of notes; a clear rhythm and a few well-chosen notes are enough.

    Start with a small note set

    Over a major-key progression, try the major pentatonic: scale degrees 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6. In C, that is C, D, E, G, A.

    Land on chord notes

    On strong beats, aim for a note in the chord currently playing. Use nearby scale notes to lead into it.

    Over C major, E or G will sound settled. Over G major, try B or D.

    Repeat and vary a motif

    Make a short musical sentence, repeat it, then change its ending or rhythm. A tiny idea that develops is easier to follow than a stream of random notes.

    Use rhythm and silence

    Leave space between phrases. Try a few long notes, then a short rhythmic answer. Silence is part of the melody too.

    Simple practice: loop I - V - vi - IV in C, improvise using C, D, E, G, A, and finish a phrase on C or E.

    A beginner practice path

    Learn one idea, play it, and listen. Short, regular practice beats trying to learn everything at once.

    1. Find C and play the C major scale slowly, saying each note name.
    2. Build C, F, and G major, then A minor, and notice which notes are shared.
    3. Play C - G - Am - F with an even count of four beats per chord.
    4. Hum a two-bar melody, then find it on the piano using C, D, E, G, and A.
    5. Change one note or rhythm and listen to how the mood shifts.
